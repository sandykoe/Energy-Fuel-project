Geoeconomics of Asia Energy Security Final Project

Explanatory Essay: Where to Refuel: Fuel Tankering as a Lens on Jet Fuel Security in Asia

Schwarzman College, Tsinghua University

Team Members: Shankai Wei, Sandy Koenig, Gastón T. Ocampo, and Marco Kaupp

1. Introduction and research question

Our project is centered around an interview by David Kang, former Qatar Airways group executive, who described the airline paying about $3.65 a gallon for jet fuel in Doha, where a state-linked monopoly set the price, while the same fuel cost $2.95 in Dubai. According to Kang, the airline sent planes to Dubai with only minimum reserves, filled them up there, and flew back to unload the fuel into its own storage (Odd Lots summary, 2026). This strategy points to something bigger: when nearby airports charge different prices, deciding where to buy fuel becomes a problem that can be optimized. That led us to our research question. Under what conditions should an airline refuel away from its home base, and what do the resulting price gaps and fallback options tell us about jet fuel security in Asia?

2. The tool and method

ACCESS THE WEBSITE: https://energy-fuel-project.vercel.app/

Our tool is an optimization model of the refueling decision. For a route or sequence of routes, it compares buying the fuel needed for the next leg at the departure airport against "tankering": carrying extra fuel bought at a cheaper airport to avoid buying at a dearer one later.

We built an optimization model of the refueling decision, while taking into consideration that the central trade-off is that extra fuel is extra weight, and weight burns fuel. For a route or multi-leg itinerary it compares buying the next leg's fuel at the departure airport with "tankering", carrying extra fuel bought more cheaply elsewhere. Taking weight limitations into consideration, the tool computes an estimate price spread per leg. The tool does not yet account for aircraft characteristics, tank capacity, landing-weight limits and reserves, outage at certain airports, etc. but can simulate certain types of disruption. These data points would strengthen the tool and can be accesses through a subscription to Argus jet fuels. Price inputs follow the structure of commercial data, which is limited but we have managed to populate the model with a minimum viable amount of data points and estimate certain prices from existing data.

3. Where the tool is most relevant

The tool suits intra-regional short and medium-haul flights, in Asia, Europe (where most of the bibliography that we have found is focused on) and similar regions. Distance matters: Eurocontrol's simulations show the extra burn from full tankering rising from 2.21% on a 300 nautical mile sector to 4.66% on 600 nautical miles (Euro Control, 2019), so the advantage is lesss on long hauls – therefore limiting the scope of the tool. Finally, our tool is relevant for regional itineraries are not round trips and those that are. For example, an itinerary such as Singapore (SIN), Jakarta (CGK), Bangkok (BKK) and Hong Kong (HKG) lets an airline choose where along the route to buy fuel for several legs.

4. Analysis: supply and demand, and the 4Gs in the case of regional routes in Asia

Supply and demand: Our model focuses on the challenge that every airport has in ensuring its own fuel demand. In our guiding case study we saw how in the case of Doha Hamad Airport (DOH), Woqod held a domestic monopoly (Odd Lots summary, 2026). Dubai’s market was not fully open either: the government-owned ENOC Group provided more than 40% of Dubai International Airport’s jet fuel requirements (Dubai Media Office, 2023). However, the rest of the market left room for other sellers, such as Chevron, which sold at $2.95 per gallon; which allowed for the Qatar Airways tankering strategy.

Geology: Asia holds 37% of global refining output but ordinarily sources about two-thirds of its crude from the Middle East, and Middle Eastern crude yields about 60% middle distillates against roughly 40% for WTI (Investing Live, 2026). Replacing Gulf barrels therefore cuts jet and diesel output. In class we saw the same geological imbalance: Asia-Pacific has a small share of global reserves relative to consumption (Professor Liu, Class 3), making prices vary and tankering an important tool for airline profitability.

Geography. Asia lacks the integrated pipelines of Europe and North America, and its oil passes through chokepoints such as Hormuz and Malacca (Professor Liu, Class 3). Fuel reaches specific hubs by tanker, so supply is concentrated. This is particularly relevant for a future version of our tool that can compute variables such as shortages and chokeholds (live data for this is key).

Geopolitics and state-market relations. Qatar shows that insecurity is often institutional: a monopoly created the gap while cheaper fuel sat a short flight away.

As the effects of the ongoing Middle East conflict persist, our tool is of increasing relevance for airlines as they seek to keep prices under control, conflict cut the region’s jet production by an estimated 640,000 barrels per day from March to June. Refiners elsewhere partly closed the gap by raising their jet fuel yields (ATC News, 2026), but importers still depend on other countries’ choices. When China curtailed its fuel exports, Australia, Bangladesh and the Philippines were left exposed because they rely on Chinese fuel (Hydrocarbon Processing, 2026). IATA’s 28 August 2026 Chart of the Week looks at how the shock showed up in regional jet fuel prices and currencies (IATA, 2026).

Takeaways:

  • Pricing matters: the Doha-Dubai difference of $0.70 per gallon saved Qatar Airways around 19% vis a vis DOH’s price, making tankering worth it.
  • Tankering is already a documented Asian practice: Based on a study consulted for this project, of more than 27 million flights between 2019 and 2023 found that each $100 per ton increase in the fuel price gap between Mainland China and Hong Kong was associated with 16.30 additional monthly flights and 192.2 kg more carbon emissions per flight (Penny wise, emission foolish?, n.d.). What does this mean? Airlines are already tankering within Asia. In order for our tool to better help airlines save money, carbon costs, live fuel prices and disruption scenarios for hubs in the Gulf, India and East Asia should be taken into account. Once these data points are incorporated, we will be able to warn decision makers of a broader array of stressors.
  • Tankering has a cost: According to a study on European tankering within the European Union, this practice adds about 901,000 tonnes of CO2 a year in Europe (Euro Control, 2019). This is one of the reasons why adding carbon costs, live fuel prices, and disruption scenarios for hubs in the Gulf, India and East Asia would further strengthen the tool and its potential to do good.
  • Tankering is a firm-level hedge, not a national solution: Resilience ultimately depends on refining capacity, stock and supply (variables around which our class revolved around), which is why strengthening the model with other data points is of essence.

5. Conclusion

Fuel security has a firm-level dimension as well as a national one: where airlines choose to buy fuel shows where a region’s supply is expensive, concentrated or fragile. Our tool is a first version built with a minimum amount of data. It shows that tankering can pay when price gaps are large and sectors are short, but it does not yet include aircraft characteristics, tank and landing-weight limits, reserves or airport outages. Next, with fuller data (for example through an Argus subscription), we would add these, together with carbon costs, live fuel prices and disruption scenarios for hubs in the Gulf, India and East Asia. Tankering is a hedge for airlines, not a national solution, so these additions would also help show policymakers where supply is under stress.
